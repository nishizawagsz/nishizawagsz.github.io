Who I am professionally

Four roles,
one person

Teaching and librarianship are the same work. The teacher stands close to the learner, the librarian a step further away.

Librarian. Teacher.
Examiner. Developer.

In about twelve years in classrooms and five years in school libraries I have learned this: a teacher shapes information and hands it over directly. A librarian shapes the collection, the space and the guidance that help others find and use it. Examining and developing digital tools are the same craft again, from two more sides.

If I had to draw the four, the librarian would be the shelves, the teacher the open book, the examiner the lines that run between claims, and the developer the grid underneath. They meet in one place, and that is where information turns into a person.

Underneath all four is a linguist. My PhD is in cognitive semantics, and Lakoff and Johnson's Metaphors We Live By shaped how I think about learning: we understand new things through the pictures we already carry. A library is a map, an essay is a journey, and a book has a home. I teach with those pictures on purpose, and I design information so that the picture is the first thing a learner sees.

Where deep learning begins

Stories change us

One story, seen from both ends

Why do we read? Reading is the first and hardest way a child learns to take in information that someone else has shaped: to hold a line of thought, to follow it when it turns, to notice when something does not make sense. Everything we later call research, critical thinking or academic integrity grows from this ability to receive information carefully. It is not a technique that can be taught in Grade 11, but a habit that forms much earlier, and the library is where it forms.

Read on: what this looks like from kindergarten to the Diploma

Working in a Kindergarten and Junior School library has made this very real for me. In the early years the library lesson belongs to the unit of inquiry: the children arrive with a question from the classroom, and the books are where the inquiry continues. A child who has been read to, who chooses books freely and may put them down again, and who has learned that a question can lead into a book, is already practising the real thing. By the Diploma years, patience with a difficult text and the habit of checking where a claim comes from are not new demands. They grow out of something that already feels natural. Without that foundation, the same demands feel like a wall.

As a Diploma teacher and as an examiner I have seen the end of this road, and now I see its beginning every day. It is the same story. That is why I think of elementary and secondary library work as one job seen from two ends: from the picture-book corner to the Extended Essay.

In practice

What I can do for students, parents and teachers

Everything I do comes back to this: what a reader holds in their hands, and how it changes them.

For children and students

  • Find the book that makes a reluctant reader come back for another one.
  • Turn a vague topic into a question that can be researched, and tell a reliable source from one that only sounds reliable.
  • Cite, reference and declare AI use without fear, because they understand why it matters, and get real support with the Personal Project and the Extended Essay.
  • For the youngest: learn where books live, with a story and a small robot hamster. That is Happy Hamster, the kindergarten library programme built on my own picture-book stories.

For parents

  • Know what to read with a child at each age, and when to let them choose alone.
  • Keep reading alive in the family language as well as in English. I raise two multilingual children myself.
  • Understand what the school expects about research, referencing and AI, so that conversations about homework become easier.
  • Honest advice about screens, audiobooks and e-books, and about keeping a teenager reading when school reading stops being fun.

For teachers

  • Plan research tasks together, so that the library lesson and the unit are one thing, not two.
  • Source packs, reading lists and research guides made for one specific unit, as part of a shared plan for research skills across the years.
  • Clear, age-appropriate rules about citation and declaring AI use, and an examiner's eye on student research before it is submitted.
  • Short, practical staff sessions about digital tools, AI and information literacy. I have designed and led such workshops before.

Approach

How I think about research and information literacy

Four working principles. They come from supervising Extended Essays, teaching secondary students and reading student portfolios as an examiner.

1Research is a continuum, not a unit

Judging sources in Grade 6 and defending an argument in the Extended Essay are the same skill at different levels. A library programme works when librarians and teachers agree what students should be able to do at each stage, and build towards it together.

2Citation is thinking made visible

Referencing is not a formatting task. It shows where an idea came from, how it changed, and what the student added. Taught this way, it becomes the base of academic honesty, not a rule to enforce.

3AI: use it, name it, stay the author

Students will use AI tools. The useful question is: for what, and how is it declared? As an examiner I see what undeclared AI use looks like. I would rather teach students to work openly than let them discover the problem at the wrong moment.

4The librarian plans with teachers

In twelve years of teaching I rarely had a librarian in the room when I set a research task, and I felt that gap every time. The library lesson I want to give is the one I wanted to receive: the teacher brings the unit and the question, the librarian brings the sources, the search strategies and the integrity checks, and both of them are in the room.

Library pedagogy · Kindergarten

Happy Hamster

Screen-free, robot-assisted library lessons for the youngest readers

“Everybody in this country should learn how to program a computer… because it teaches you how to think.”

Steve Jobs, in What most schools don’t teach (Code.org, 2013). He also called computer science a liberal art, something everybody should learn. Happy Hamster starts that on the kindergarten carpet.
  • A library programme for kindergarten children, built on my own picture-book stories. The first story, Mr Hamster and the Crying Book, is about a little blue book that a child took off the shelf, looked at and left on the floor, and about the hamster who lives on the librarian's desk and helps the book find its way home.
More about the lesson and the robot
  • After the story, the children do tasks with Story-Bot, Hammy's robot friend (a TaleBot Pro). They guide it to bring the lost book back to its place, next to its friend. The first theme is simple: a book you take off the shelf has a home, and you can take it back there.
  • This is robot-assisted learning, not robotics. The children do not build the robot and do not learn to code. They tell Story-Bot, step by step, how to get from the reading corner to the right shelf. That is a first, playful algorithm, and its purpose is to teach logical thinking at an age when it still feels like a game. The whole lesson is screen-free: a book, a story, a robot on the carpet and the children's own words. The robot is a helper in the lesson, a friend of the hamster from the story, and a patient one. The other aim is that a four-year-old leaves the library wanting the next book.

Library pedagogy · Grades 11 to 12

Stay the Author!

Research writing in the age of AI, for the oldest library users

  • Stay the Author! is the older brother of Happy Hamster. The kindergarten programme is about a book finding its way home, and this one is about a student who remains the author of her own essay, even when machines offer to help.
  • I know this road from both ends. As an IB Diploma teacher I supervised Extended Essays myself, and as a portfolio examiner I now read what students hand in. AI use and questions of authorship have become my special interest. As a librarian, I want to pass what I have learned from real student work on to the next generation, before they hand their own work in.
More about the four stations
  • Four stations on the way to the Extended Essay, each one a short library workshop and a one-to-one meeting: Question (finding it and making it smaller), Sources (searching, judging, citing), Voice (your own sentences, and how AI use is declared), Ownership (which parts of your text you can defend before you hand it in).
  • This is not a ban on AI and not a plagiarism police, but a librarian who helps students stay the authors of their own work.

Stay the Author!

Using AI in the Extended Essay · Grades 11 to 12

The IB does not ban AI tools. Its position is that any AI-generated text, image or graph you include must be credited in the body of your essay and referenced in the bibliography, and that the IB "does not regard any work produced, even only in part, by such tools, to be their own". In other words: you can quote it and cite it, but it will not count as your work. Your school's academic integrity and AI policies and your supervisor's guidance always come first. Check both before you start.

Where AI can help

  • Exploring angles on a topic before you choose a research question
  • Explaining a difficult concept you have met in your reading
  • Checking the grammar of a paragraph you wrote
  • Generating practice questions to test your understanding
  • Summarising an article you have already read, to check you understood it

Where it will let you down

  • Finding sources. AI invents citations, so verify every reference in a real database or catalogue.
  • Writing your paragraphs. The essay must be your argument in your words.
  • Analysing your data or your primary source for you
  • Replacing the reading. Summaries miss what makes a source worth citing.
Read the full guide: how to acknowledge AI use, the checklist and the sources

How to acknowledge AI use

  1. If AI-generated text appears in your essay, put it in quotation marks and cite it in the text and in the bibliography.
  2. If you used an AI idea or explanation, paraphrase it and cite it the same way.
  3. Record the tool and version, the date, the URL (of the conversation if you can) and the prompt, for example: ChatGPT (GPT-5), OpenAI, 12 Oct 2026, prompt: "Explain the difference between…", https://chatgpt.com/...
  4. Keep a short AI log alongside your research log. Your supervisor may ask about it in the reflection sessions.
Before you submit, ask yourself:Could I explain every claim in this essay without the tool?
SourcesHave I opened and read every source in my bibliography myself?
AcknowledgementIs every piece of AI help I relied on visible to the reader?

Programme ideas

Reading culture in a school library

Ideas I keep returning to. Some come from what I see working now, some I would like to try with students and colleagues.

01

Home and Family Languages shelf with parent notes

A visible, well-chosen section for the languages that families really speak at home, with short reading notes for parents in those languages. As a Hungarian-speaking parent in Japan I know how much one familiar shelf can mean, and how rarely parents are told what to do with it.

02

Morning book talks in short video clips

Short video clips as morning book talks, made to fit what subject teachers ask for. They can be played at the start of a lesson as a warm-up and a way into the topic, in a partnership between the subject teacher and the librarian.

03

Reading sessions for reluctant and developing readers

Regular, relaxed reading sessions for Middle School, built around choice: graphic novels, audiobooks, short fiction and non-fiction in several languages, so that the first step is never a page full of text. It brings the freedom of the Junior School library corner into the secondary years.

04

Author and translator visits

Inviting translators together with authors turns a reading event into a conversation about language itself. It suits a multilingual school, and it shows students that reading in more than one language is a skill worth celebrating.

Languages · AI · Cognitive linguistics

Why learn languages when AI can translate everything?

After five months in Japan with a translation app in my pocket, I noticed something: the apps translated every sign, and I still felt a constant, quiet stress. My brain, unable to process the language around me, had built a wall. AI opens a window into a culture, but it does not tear down that wall for you. Drawing on my research in cognitive linguistics, I argue for language teaching that builds linguistic agency, the freedom to take part, not just to understand. It starts with fifteen unfiltered minutes a day.

Physical computing · Educators' workshop

When the tsunami comes, there is no time to close the door

At a robotics workshop for educators, a colleague and I built a working model of an automated tsunami rescue capsule from an Artec kit: a sensor picks up the alarm, a servo motor closes and locks the door. This is what I like most about physical computing. The code does not just run on a screen, it makes something happen in the real world. And living in Japan, a tsunami is not a textbook example.

Programming · Elementary school

What if you could build your own robot in half an hour?

A cardboard robot changes who wants to program. The body is paper: light, safe, cheap, and decorated by the child, so the same kit becomes a cat, a squirrel or a dinosaur. The motor and the sensors go inside, and the children program it with blocks, like Scratch, but with code that is easier to read and to extend later. Half an hour, one robot, and a room of eight-year-olds who think programming is theirs.
